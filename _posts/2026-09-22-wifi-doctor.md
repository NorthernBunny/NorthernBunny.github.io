Made a tool to read in wifi logs generated by OS X wireless diagnostics. Here are the notes.
{:.lead}

## What it does

**wifi-doctor** is a command-line tool that ingests logs from macOS Wireless Diagnostics and produces a plain-English summary of what went wrong with the network, the environment, or the client.

Point it at a Wireless Diagnostics bundle, a `wifi.log`, unified log output, or a `wdutil` snapshot, and it decodes IEEE 802.11 reason and status codes, identifies patterns like roaming storms and auth timeouts, and explains each problem in a way that points toward a fix rather than just naming the error.

The motivation is simple: macOS produces genuinely good wireless diagnostics, and then buries them in log formats that take an expert to read. The information needed to diagnose a bad Wi-Fi experience is usually already sitting in that bundle — it's just unreadable at a glance.

## Input formats

| Format | Looks like |
|---|---|
| WirelessDiagnostics bundle | `WirelessDiagnostics-*.tar.gz` |
| Classic `wifi.log` | `Tue Dec 15 13:21:12.857 ...` |
| Unified log output | `log show --predicate 'process == "airportd"'` |
| `wdutil` snapshot | `sudo wdutil info > snapshot.txt` |

An extracted bundle folder works too — it'll walk the directory and find what it recognises.

## Usage

```bash
python3 wifi_doctor.py ~/Downloads/WirelessDiagnostics-2026-09-16.tar.gz
python3 wifi_doctor.py wifi.log wdutil_info.txt
python3 wifi_doctor.py extracted_bundle_folder/
```

| Option | Effect |
|---|---|
| `--verbose` | Print the full event timeline |
| `--json` | Machine-readable JSON output |
| `--window 5` | Roam-burst detection window in minutes (default 10) |

It's **pure Python 3 with no external dependencies** — no `pip install` step, nothing to set up in a virtualenv. That was a deliberate constraint: a diagnostic tool that needs its own installation ritual doesn't get used in the field.

## What it detects

### Critical

| Finding | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Excessive roaming** | The client roamed frequently — measured both as a rate per hour and as a burst inside a window. Usually overlapping APs at similar signal levels, too-aggressive minimum-RSSI steering, or a sticky client sitting at the cell edge |
| **Auth failures** | Association or authentication rejected with IEEE 802.11 status codes — status 17 is "AP full," status 43 is "steering away." Generally means the AP won't accept this client |
| **WPA handshake timeouts** | The 4-way handshake failed or timed out (reason 15 or 16). Points at a wrong or changed password, a PSK mismatch, or a WPA3/WPA2 compatibility problem |
| **802.1X failures** | Enterprise auth (EAPOL) rejected by the network. Points at the RADIUS server, the certificates, or the user and machine credentials |
| **DHCP timeouts** | Layer 2 connects but no IP arrives. Check the DHCP scope, VLAN tagging, and client isolation settings |

### Warning

| Finding | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Slow roams** | A roam took over 2 seconds — long enough to drop a call. Often missing 802.11k/r/v support, or a full 802.1X re-auth on every roam |
| **Beacon loss** | The client lost all beacons from the AP. Severe interference, an AP reboot or DFS radar event, or the client simply walking out of range |
| **Low SNR** | Signal-to-noise ratio below 20 dB, from the logged RSSI and noise figures. Expect data-rate fallback and an unstable link |
| **Weak signal** | RSSI consistently at or below −75 dBm across many samples. The client is on the cell edge, which drives retransmissions and roams |
| **Congested channel** | High channel utilisation, CCA above 40%. Expect latency and jitter; a quieter channel is the usual answer |

```
✖ [CRITICAL] Excessive roaming
    The Mac roamed between access points 12 times (~11.0/hour);
    worst burst was 12 roams within 10 min. Frequent roaming
    usually means overlapping APs at similar signal levels,
    too-high minimum-RSSI/steering settings, or the client at
    a cell edge.

✖ [CRITICAL] Security handshake timeouts
    1 disconnect(s) with a 4-way/group key handshake timeout.
    This usually means a wrong/changed Wi-Fi password, a PSK
    mismatch, or an AP/controller dropping EAPOL frames.

▲ [WARNING ] Poor channel conditions
    1 disconnect(s) citing missed ACKs/poor channel (reason 34)
    — points to RF interference or weak coverage.

▲ [WARNING ] Weak signal (possible sticky client / cell edge)
    5 of 5 signal samples were at or below -75 dBm
    (average -79 dBm). The Mac spent significant time at the
    edge of coverage, which drives retransmissions and roams.

• [INFO    ] Connected on 2.4 GHz
    The Mac is on channel 2g11/20 (2.4 GHz), which is slower
    and more interference-prone. If 5/6 GHz is available,
    check band-steering and signal coverage.
```

That output is the whole point of the exercise. Each finding states the observation, the likely causes in order, and where to look next — which is what turns a log file into a next action.

## How it works

Nothing clever or proprietary is going on. It leans on three publicly documented things:

* **IEEE 802.11 reason and status codes** — the standard's deauthentication, disassociation and authentication reasons. Reason 15 is a handshake timeout, reason 23 is an 802.1X rejection, reason 34 is poor channel conditions, and so on.
* **Apple's log patterns** — the well-known `airportd`, `eapolclient` and kernel messages: "Roaming started," "BEACONS LOST," "802.1X: authentication failed."
* **Signal metrics** — RSSI, CCA (channel utilisation), noise and TX rate, pulled from `wdutil info` output or from log entries directly.

The whole thing is a single file of roughly 700 lines. Everything is read locally and nothing is uploaded anywhere, which matters given that a diagnostics bundle contains a fair amount about a network and the devices on it.

## Design notes

A few decisions that turned out to matter:

**Severity has to be honest.** It's tempting to flag everything, but a tool that marks ten things critical has said nothing at all. Excessive roaming and a failed handshake are genuinely different in kind from "the client is on 2.4 GHz," and sorting them into critical, warning and info is most of the tool's value.

**Findings need thresholds, not just pattern matches.** "The client roamed" isn't a finding. "The client roamed 12 times in an hour, with 12 of those inside one 10-minute window" is, because it's comparable against what normal looks like. That's also why the burst window is a command-line option — what counts as a storm depends on the environment.

**Plain English beats precision, up to a point.** The output says "a wrong or changed Wi-Fi password, a PSK mismatch, or an AP dropping EAPOL frames" rather than "reason code 15." The reason code is still there for anyone who wants it, but the sentence is what gets read.

**No dependencies was the right constraint.** It's a field tool. Anything that needs a virtualenv gets left on the laptop that isn't in the bag.

## Where it's going

Not published yet — right now it's a single script rather than a repository. The next step is putting it on GitHub with the usage notes above as its README, so it's something other people can actually clone and run. Ideas queued up after that:

* A **timeline view** that plots roams, disconnects and RSSI on one axis, since the shape of a problem over time is often more legible than a list of findings
* **More vendor-specific log patterns** — the Apple side is well covered, but controller-side logs from the AP end tell the other half of the story
* **Comparison mode**, to diff a good session against a bad one on the same network, which is frequently the fastest route to a cause
